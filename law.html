What the law says, and what it can mean

The New York statutes, the cases that interpret them, Cornell's own policy, and what each can mean in this case. Not legal advice.

Updated Oct 2, 2026, 2:30 pm Eastern

Confirmed primary document or acknowledged Alleged one side's claim, unproven Disputed the parties contradict Open unanswered How labels work

Two different rulebooks apply to the same night. Cornell's disciplinary process uses the campus definition of consent required by New York's Education Law and decides cases on a preponderance of the evidence. The criminal case uses the Penal Law's narrower definition of lack of consent and requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt at trial. The civil lawsuit uses the preponderance standard and does not require a criminal conviction. This is how the same conduct could produce expulsions at Cornell, no charges from the DA, and a 101-page lawsuit.

Consent under the Penal Law

Lack of consent is an element of every sex offense in New York (Penal Law § 130.05). It can be shown by forcible compulsion, by incapacity to consent, or, for certain offenses, by the victim's clearly expressed refusal. The definitions that matter here:

"Mentally incapacitated" means that a person is rendered temporarily incapable of appraising or controlling his conduct owing to the influence of a narcotic or intoxicating substance administered to him without his consent, or to any other act committed upon him without his consent.

Penal Law § 130.00(6)

"Physically helpless" means that a person is unconscious or for any other reason is physically unable to communicate unwillingness to an act.

Penal Law § 130.00(7)

[For rape in the third degree under § 130.25(7) to (9), lack of consent also exists under] circumstances under which, at the time of the act ... the victim clearly expressed that he or she did not consent to engage in such act, and a reasonable person in the actor's situation would have understood such person's words and acts as an expression of lack of consent to such act under all the circumstances.

Penal Law § 130.05(2)(d)

Where lack of consent rests only on incapacity, it is an affirmative defense that the defendant "did not know of the facts or conditions responsible for such incapacity to consent" (§ 130.10(1)).

How New York courts have read these: in People v. Johnson, 23 N.Y.3d 973 (2014), the Court of Appeals held that a victim's voluntary intoxication does not make her "mentally incapacitated," because the statute requires a substance "administered ... without his consent." In People v. Bjork, 105 A.D.3d 1258 (3d Dept 2013), the court stated that a person "who is asleep or unable to communicate as a result of voluntary intoxication is considered to be physically helpless." People v. Shevchenko, 175 A.D.3d 1010 (4th Dept 2019), upheld a first-degree rape conviction on a physically-helpless theory where the victim had drunk voluntarily and was also given ketamine. Confirmed

What it can mean

If she took the ketamine knowingly, the "mentally incapacitated" category likely does not apply, which is the point the DA made in 2024 and the gap lawmakers are now discussing. The remaining routes to a felony are "physically helpless" (evidence she was unable to communicate unwillingness during a given act, which supports first-degree rape and has no statute of limitations) and "clearly expressed" refusal (evidence that an act continued after she said no, which supports third-degree rape). The 1:42 am message and later replies are relevant to what the members knew about her condition, because the § 130.10 defense turns on knowledge; the respondents' statements to Cornell that she seemed "perfectly normal" and "didn't seem inebriated at all" are the other side of that same question. The DA's 2024 reading was that every act she refused stopped; the question a grand jury would weigh is whether that is true of every act described.

The pending change. New York is one of 19 states whose law treats intoxication as incapacity only when the substance was taken without the person's knowledge or consent. A bill to change the definition passed the State Senate unanimously in June 2026 and has not been taken up by the Assembly (reported as S.10632/A.101 by TIME and amNY, and as S.54 by City & State; earlier versions passed the Senate in 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024 and 2025). Minnesota (2021), Michigan (2023) and Texas (2024) have made similar changes. The Assembly working group formed Sept 30 is to report by year end, and Gov. Hochul said on Oct 2, "I support closing this loophole, and will work with the New York state Legislature to accomplish this." A change in the law would not apply to conduct in 2024. Confirmed

Source: TIME, Sept 30, 2026

Consent under Cornell's policy and the Education Law

Affirmative consent is a knowing, voluntary, and mutual decision among all participants to engage in sexual activity. Consent can be given by words or actions, as long as those words or actions create clear permission regarding willingness to engage in the sexual activity. Silence or lack of resistance, in and of itself, does not demonstrate consent.

Education Law § 6441(1), which every New York college must adopt

Cornell's Policy 6.4 procedures add: "Someone who is under the influence of alcohol, drugs, or other intoxicants may be incapacitated and therefore unable to consent depending on the level of intoxication," and ask whether the respondent knew, or a sober reasonable person should have known, that the complainant was incapacitated. The standard of proof is preponderance, "more likely than not." Policy 6.4 also defines separate offenses, including "sexual exploitation," which covers, among other things, "observing another person when that person is nude or engaged in sexual activity without the knowledge and consent of the person observed" and "causing another person to become incapacitated with the intent of making that person vulnerable to nonconsensual sexual assault or sexual exploitation"; an attempt, meaning conduct "directly tending toward completion of the prohibited conduct" with intent, is itself a violation (procedures §§ 3.2, 3.6). That is the category one respondent was found to have attempted, per the court records of his challenge. Article 129-B also grants amnesty from campus alcohol and drug charges to students who report sexual assault (§ 6442), and states that it creates no private right of action (§ 6440(9)). Confirmed

Under Cornell's procedures, the parties and their advisors receive the full investigative record and report at least ten days before a hearing (§ 24.8), so several people on each side held copies. The DA said on Sept 30 that Cornell might resist sharing the interview transcript on the ground that it was confidential Title IX material, and said on Oct 1 that he had obtained it. Which party or office the press copies came from has not been reported. Confirmed

What it can mean

Cornell's panel could find a student responsible for sex with someone incapacitated by her own voluntary drinking and drug use; the Penal Law's "mentally incapacitated" category could not reach the same conduct. A Cornell finding of responsibility is therefore not evidence that a crime occurred, and a Cornell finding of not responsible is not evidence that one did not. The lawsuit's standalone Education Law claim faces § 6440(9), although Cornell's compliance with its own procedures can still matter to a breach of contract claim.

Statutes of limitation

Under CPL § 30.10: first-degree rape may be charged at any time; second-degree rape within 20 years; third-degree rape within 10 years; most other felonies within 5 years; misdemeanors, which include sexual misconduct, forcible touching and third-degree sexual abuse, within 2 years. Confirmed

What it can mean

For conduct on Oct 19 to 20, 2024, the two-year window for misdemeanor charges closes on Oct 19 to 20, 2026, unless a tolling rule applies (for example, time a defendant was continuously outside New York). The DA's estimated timing, "within about 45 days" of Sept 28, falls after that date. Charges presented after it would need to be felonies.

The grand jury, and who presents to it

Under Executive Law § 63(2), the Governor may require the Attorney General to take over any criminal matter from a local district attorney. That happened here on Oct 1: Executive Order 64 makes the Attorney General special prosecutor and "displace[s] and supersede[s]" the Tompkins County DA, with authority to investigate, "present evidence to a grand jury," and prosecute. Confirmed

A grand jury may indict when "the evidence before it is legally sufficient to establish that such person committed such offense" and "competent and admissible evidence before it provides reasonable cause to believe" it (CPL § 190.65). At least twelve grand jurors must agree. The DA decides what evidence and charges to present, and the grand jury may not take legal advice from anyone but the DA and the court. A person whose case is being presented has a right to testify before the grand jury after waiving immunity (CPL § 190.50(5)). An indictment is an accusation, not a finding of guilt. Confirmed

What it can mean

"Reasonable cause" is a much lower bar than proof beyond a reasonable doubt. A grand jury can indict on evidence that would not convict at trial, and the DA's public statements that the 2024 facts did not support charges will be available to the defense if a case goes to trial.

The civil case

Civil claims for conduct constituting a listed sex offense may be brought within 20 years, and "nothing in this section shall be construed to require that a criminal charge be brought or a criminal conviction be obtained" (CPLR § 213-c). Other intentional torts have a one-year period and negligence three years. The standard is preponderance of the evidence. Confirmed

On the claims against institutions: New York does not treat colleges as standing in the place of parents (Eiseman v. State of New York, 70 N.Y.2d 175 (1987)), and courts have generally declined to impose a duty on colleges to protect students from other students. Courts have found a duty where a school had notice of ongoing dangerous conduct at a fraternity it controlled (Brown v. University of Rochester, 3d Dept 2023; Tsialas v. Cornell University, Sup. Ct. Tompkins County 2020, a hazing death case in which Cornell's control over Greek life and its houses supported the claims). National fraternity organizations are generally not liable for a chapter's conduct absent day-to-day control (Parslow v. Leake, 4th Dept 2014). The Dram Shop Act (GOL § 11-100) covers furnishing alcohol to a person under 21, but under Sheehy v. Big Flats Community Day, 73 N.Y.2d 629 (1989), the intoxicated person cannot recover for injuries caused by her own intoxication. Confirmed

What it can mean

The claims most likely to turn on contested facts are the battery and assault claims against the individual members. The claims against Cornell depend heavily on what Cornell knew about the house before Oct 19, 2024, which is why the "ketamine culture" allegation and the alleged police response a week earlier matter. The claims against Moonies and the sorority face established limits on recovering for one's own intoxication. Expect motions to dismiss to narrow the case before any trial.

What Cornell may disclose

Nothing in this section shall be construed to prohibit an institution of postsecondary education from disclosing the final results of any disciplinary proceeding conducted by such institution against a student who is an alleged perpetrator of any crime of violence ... or a nonforcible sex offense, if the institution determines as a result of that disciplinary proceeding that the student committed a violation of the institution's rules or policies with respect to such crime or offense.

20 U.S.C. § 1232g(b)(6)(B) (FERPA). "Final results" means the student's name, the violation, and any sanction.

The Clery Act separately requires a school to notify both the accuser and the accused, in writing and simultaneously, of the outcome of a disciplinary proceeding for a sex offense. Confirmed

What it can mean

Federal law permits, but does not require, a university to name the students it found responsible for a sex offense and the sanctions imposed. It does not permit disclosure about students found not responsible. Cornell's statement that privacy law "prohibits" disclosure is accurate as to the latter group and a choice as to the former.

Statements about the case

Statements made in court filings are absolutely privileged against defamation claims, and fair and true reports of court filings are protected by Civil Rights Law § 74 (Gottwald v. Sebert, 40 N.Y.3d 240 (2023)). Statements made outside court, including to the press and online, are not. Accusing a named person of a serious crime is defamation per se if false.
