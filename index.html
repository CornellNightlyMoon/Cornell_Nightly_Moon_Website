What is actually known about the Chi Phi case.

The 2024 sexual assault case at Cornell's Chi Phi fraternity house, laid out from the primary documents: a timeline, her own accounts side by side, what is confirmed, what is only alleged, what is disputed, and what New York law says.

Written by one person, independently and anonymously. Not affiliated with Cornell, any party to the case, or any news organization. No opinions on guilt or liability, no names of the accused, every claim labeled and sourced, and the court index number so you can check the file yourself. One section is opinion, about the coverage, and is labeled as such.

Where things stand

As of Oct 2, 2026, 2 pm Eastern

James on Oct 2: "This is now an active criminal investigation led by the office of the attorney general"; "if warranted, pursue prosecutions"; "a comprehensive investigation of this nature takes time." No grand jury date exists. Confirmed
  • The DA accepted the appointment and said: "It has become clear that we were not provided the full picture by the University." Cornell has not said whether the 120-page interview transcript was ever sent. Confirmed
  • Records from Cornell's Title IX case, and from one defendant's court challenge to his finding, have been obtained and reported by The New York Times, CBS, ABC, NBC and CNN between Sept 29 and Oct 2: interview transcripts, a 99-page written decision, texts, the full group chat including a photo of her in the house, and 2025 hearing testimony. They are summarized under What the Title IX records add. Confirmed

The short version

On the night of Oct 19, 2024, a 20-year-old Cornell student went to the Chi Phi fraternity house at 107 Edgemoor Lane, on campus, after drinking at her sorority house and at two bars. On Nov 8, 2024 she reported to the Cornell University Police Department (CUPD) that she had been sexually assaulted there by multiple fraternity members after taking ketamine. Cornell suspended the chapter and the accused students the same day, opened a formal Title IX investigation in January 2025, held hearings in May 2025, and imposed sanctions it describes as "expulsions and suspensions."

In November 2024, CUPD asked the District Attorney to review her six-page written statement. The DA concluded that the facts in it, as written, did not meet New York's criminal definition of lack of consent, and no charges were filed. On Sept 14, 2026 she filed a 101-page civil lawsuit against 17 defendants, alleging she was drugged and gang-raped by seven members over about four hours and that Cornell, the fraternity, her sorority and a bar were negligent. The lawsuit's allegations are more severe than, and in places inconsistent with, her 2024 written statement; an interview transcript from the same week, published Sept 30, 2026, contains statements the DA says he never received.

The DA reopened the case on Sept 28, 2026. Between Sept 29 and Oct 2, records from Cornell's Title IX investigation, and from one defendant's court challenge to his finding, reached five national news organizations. They show her telling roommates about the night without calling it rape, texting one of the men two days later that "non of the sexual stuff was illegal," texting the same man two weeks later that "90% of what happened that night was not consensual," requesting a rape kit on Nov 7, telling police that same week "I can say with 100% confidence I was raped," and testifying at the 2025 hearing that "I felt like bait. I felt like lions around me." They also contain the accused men's accounts, most of which say she did not appear intoxicated, and the full group chat, including a photo of her in the house. On Oct 1 the Governor removed the DA from the case and appointed the Attorney General as special prosecutor, saying the words in her police interview "never made it into the account that the Cornell police sent to prosecutors." Cornell has agreed to an outside review, the Attorney General is separately reviewing Cornell's response, and the State Assembly is reviewing the law. The central factual questions are what she told police in 2024 versus what the complaint alleges, and what CUPD passed on to the DA. The central legal issue is how New York defines consent when the person was voluntarily intoxicated.

The campus paper has covered the reaction, not the record

Opinion The author's view. This is the one section of this site that is opinion rather than record. Every fact it relies on is in the coverage audit, with a link to each article checked.

For two weeks The Cornell Daily Sun has reported who is angry about this case. It has not reported what the case is.

Count what the paper most of campus relies on has published: 47 items on Chi Phi since November 2024, 31 of them since the lawsuit was reported on Sept 18. Every official statement is there, often first. So is every politician, every petition and every protest, plus ten opinion pieces: an editorial, a letter from the editor, a guest column, three letters to the editor, two columns, a faculty statement and a reader-response feature. What is not there is a single news story on the documents. As of Oct 2, 1:30 pm, a reader who gets this story only from the Sun has never been told:

  • What her November 2024 written statement said, in either direction: that she agreed to the first encounter "due to how attracted [she] was," was "pretty ok" with it, and said "Sure" to more ketamine; and that she "felt like coercion," said "Hell No," was "instantly horrified," "completely numb," and "unable to make any decisions because of how intoxicated I was."
  • That the police interview transcript records her saying "I can say with 100% confidence I was raped"; that the DA says he never received it; that Cornell quietly rewrote its FAQ to list only the sworn statement and the screenshot as what it sent; and that the DA, hours before he was removed, said "we were not provided the full picture by the University."
  • President Kotlikoff's breakdown of the sanctions: two expelled, two suspended, one graduated, two found not responsible.
  • That two of the seven say the university found them not responsible for any sexual misconduct, that one has a hair-follicle test showing no ketamine, and that the complaint accuses four of the seven of intercourse, not seven.
  • Her texts in the days after: "non of the sexual stuff was illegal" on Oct 21, "90% of what happened that night was not consensual" on Nov 3. The roommates' accounts. The Cornell Health visit where no rape kit was offered, and her request for one 19 days later.
  • Her hearing testimony ("I felt like bait. I felt like lions around me"), the men's own accounts to Cornell's investigators, the full group chat, the photo of her in the room, the ketamine overdose at the house the day before, and the public court file in which one of the seven challenged his finding and lost.
  • The New York rule that voluntary intoxication is not legal incapacity, which is the reason the DA gave for not charging in 2024, and the Senate bill to change it that the Governor endorsed on Oct 2.

Every item on that list was reported first by someone else: CBS, ABC, The New York Times, NBC, CNN. None of it was hard to get. Part of it sits in a public court file a few blocks from the Sun's office. On Oct 2 the Governor of New York stood at a podium and quoted the transcript, "100% confident," as her reason for taking the case away from the district attorney. The Sun's story on that appointment, published the same morning, does not mention the transcript.

What the Sun did publish matters because of what it left out. On Sept 21, three days after the complaint was reported, with one defense lawyer's denial on the record and nothing else, the editorial board printed seven names under the words "alleging they drugged and gang raped a Cornell student" and closed with: "We know who you are and we refuse to let you benefit from this University's concealment." The complaint accuses four of the seven of intercourse. The university's own count has two of the seven found not responsible for sexual misconduct, and the court file shows one of the seven was found responsible for nothing more than walking into the room and replying to the chat. The editorial has not been revised, annotated, or followed by a word about the difference. On Sept 29 the Sun published a letter stating that "five others were allowed to write essays," 26 hours after its own news desk had reported Cornell's denial; the letter has been edited twice since, the second time without a note, and the sentence stands. The Sept 18 lawsuit story still says the Ithaca police are investigating; the Ithaca police say they were never told. Three 2025 news stories date the incident to a day that the complaint, the DA and Cornell all contradict. No correction has run on anything.

The harm is concrete. Two of the seven men named in that editorial say the university found them not responsible for sexual misconduct; the university's count has two such findings; the court file shows one of them was found responsible for the attempted offense of entering the room and replying to the chat, and nothing more. The paper that named them to the whole campus has told its readers none of it. A campus has spent two weeks organizing around a version of the sanctions, "essays," that the record does not support for the men found responsible for the assault, and a member of Congress repeated it at an Ithaca town hall. And the complainant is not served either. The Sun's readers have never read her strongest words, the ones the Governor quoted, because the Sun has never printed them.

This is not bias toward one side. The record the Sun has left out contains the things that complicate the complaint, the things that support it, and the things that separate the seven men from each other, and the Sun has printed none of the three. It is a decision to cover the controversy instead of the case. On a story that is, at bottom, about whether the institutions in Ithaca told the whole truth about what they knew, a newspaper that does the same thing is part of the story.

What the Sun could do today: report the record, fact by fact, as five national outlets already have; distinguish the seven defendants in every story; annotate the Sept 21 editorial and the Sept 29 letter; correct the Sept 18 story; and tell readers which facts in the audit it has verified and which it disputes. If any row in the audit is wrong, the address for corrections is at the bottom of every page, and the row will be fixed the same day.

How to read this site
