What can happen next

The criminal case, the civil case, the reviews of Cornell, the legislature, and the people involved: what each path can produce and what it would take. Possibilities, not predictions.

Updated Oct 2, 2026, 2:30 pm Eastern

Confirmed primary document or acknowledged Alleged one side's claim, unproven Disputed the parties contradict Open unanswered How labels work

What can happen from here, under New York law and the procedures that apply. This page describes possibilities and what each would require. It does not predict which will happen, and it is not legal advice.

Five things are moving at once and none depends on the others: the criminal case, the civil case, the reviews of Cornell, the legislature, and the people involved. Each is taken in turn.

The criminal case

Where it is

Since the evening of Oct 1 the case belongs to the New York Attorney General, appointed special prosecutor by Executive Order 64 under Executive Law § 63(2). The order displaces the Tompkins County DA and authorizes the Attorney General to investigate, "present evidence to a grand jury" and prosecute. The Attorney General's office has said it will "review the facts, gather evidence, interview witnesses, and if warranted, pursue prosecutions," that "a comprehensive investigation of this nature takes time," and that it will not focus on the DA's conduct. The DA's earlier estimate of a grand jury presentation "within about 45 days" no longer applies; the office that made it is off the case. Grand jury proceedings are secret; the public learns the result only when an indictment is filed or the prosecutor announces an outcome.

What a fresh investigation can do that the 2024 review did not: interview her directly; interview the roughly 30 witnesses Cornell's investigator spoke to; obtain the full Title IX record, including the 1,700 pages of hearing testimony in which the respondents testified under Cornell's process; subpoena phones and the complete group chat; and test the respondents' accounts that she did not appear intoxicated against the chat and the witnesses. It cannot recover physical evidence that was never collected: no rape kit was done until Nov 7, 2024.

What the grand jury can do

  • Vote no true bill. No charges. The same charge can be presented again only with a judge's permission (CPL § 190.75(3)). The prosecutor would normally announce the result.
  • Indict some or all of the men on some or all of the charges presented. At least twelve grand jurors must find the evidence legally sufficient and reasonable cause to believe each person committed each offense. Jurors decide person by person and charge by charge, so one outcome for seven people is not required.
  • Hear from the accused. Any person whose case is being presented has the right to testify before the grand jury after waiving immunity (CPL § 190.50(5)). Their lawyers cannot cross-examine other witnesses there.

If anyone is indicted

  • Arraignment in Tompkins County Court; rape charges are bail-eligible, so a judge can set bail or release conditions.
  • Motions: to dismiss for insufficient grand jury evidence; over the former DA's public statements that the 2024 facts did not support charges, which the defense can use; over the two-year delay. These rarely end a case but shape it.
  • Discovery under CPL Article 245 gives the defense the prosecution's file, including her 2024 statement and transcript, the Snapchat material, and whatever Cornell provided.
  • Plea negotiations can happen at any point. A plea to a lesser charge is the most common way a felony case ends.
  • Trial: proof beyond a reasonable doubt, before a jury, with her as the central witness and cross-examination on the 2024 statement and the texts. The prosecution's main exhibits would be the transcript, the group chat, the bruising, and the witnesses' accounts. Separate trials for separate defendants are possible.
  • Conviction for rape or first-degree sexual abuse requires registration as a sex offender. Indictment to trial typically takes a year or more.

If no one is indicted

  • The criminal track ends unless new evidence persuades a judge to allow re-presentation. The Governor has already used her one structural remedy, replacing the DA with the Attorney General.
  • Nothing about the civil case, Cornell's sanctions, or the reviews changes.

The civil case

Where it is

The complaint was filed Sept 14 in New York County. Service on the individual defendants is underway; affidavits of service for several have been filed. A defendant has 20 days from personal delivery, or 30 days otherwise, to answer or move (CPLR § 320, § 3012). The first responses are due in October and November.

What happens first: motions to dismiss

A motion to dismiss under CPLR § 3211 assumes the complaint's facts are true and asks whether the law gives a claim. Expect the institutional defendants to move rather than answer. The questions those motions would raise, by claim:

  • Education Law Article 129-B claim against Cornell. The statute says it creates no private right of action (§ 6440(9)).
  • Negligence against Cornell. New York does not treat colleges as standing in for parents. The claim can proceed if the complaint adequately alleges that Cornell knew of ongoing dangerous conduct at a house it controlled (Brown v. University of Rochester, 2023; Tsialas v. Cornell, 2020). The "ketamine culture" allegation, and the overdose at the house the day before, are what this turns on. Notice is usually a fact question that goes to discovery.
  • Human Rights Law and contract claims against Cornell. Courts apply a deliberate-indifference standard to the first and a substantial-compliance standard to the second. Cornell's same-day suspensions, months-long investigation, hearings and sanctions are its defense; the handling of the transcript and screenshot, and the mid-case change in federal Title IX rules, are the plaintiff's.
  • Chi Phi's national organization and Educational Trust. Liable only if they controlled the chapter's day-to-day conduct (Parslow v. Leake, 2014). Chi Phi's statement that chapters "manage their own affairs" is that defense.
  • The house association and chapter officers. Premises liability for a house the association owns; the officers' duty to supervise. Likely to survive the pleading stage; the question is insurance and collectability.
  • Tri Delta. No general duty to prevent an adult member's drinking, and the alleged assault happened hours later elsewhere.
  • Moonies. The Dram Shop Act covers serving someone under 21, but a person cannot recover for injuries caused by her own intoxication (Sheehy v. Big Flats, 1989), and Moonies denies serving her.
  • The individual defendants. Battery and assault claims for conduct constituting a listed sex offense have a 20-year limit (CPLR § 213-c). For the two defendants accused only of the ketamine conduct, the question is whether what is alleged against them is a listed offense; if not, the one-year limit for intentional torts, which expired in Oct 2025, applies to those claims. The negligence claim pleaded against every defendant has a three-year limit.

If claims survive: discovery

  • Cornell's complete Title IX file, CUPD's records, and possibly the DA's file become evidence. Phones and the full Snapchat history can be subpoenaed. Every party and the roughly 20 witnesses can be deposed under oath.
  • Her anonymity: she sues as Jane Doe. A defendant can ask the court to require her name; courts weigh privacy against the public interest and the defendants' ability to defend, and often allow pseudonyms in sexual assault cases, but it is not automatic.
  • If any defendant is indicted, the civil case against him is often paused until the criminal case ends. A defendant can refuse to answer deposition questions on Fifth Amendment grounds, and in a civil case the jury may draw an adverse inference from that.
  • Defendants can bring claims against each other for contribution.

How it can end

  • Dismissal of some defendants or claims at the motion stage or on summary judgment.
  • Settlement by any defendant at any time, with or without admission, confidential or not. Insurance, for the house association, the fraternity and the university, is usually what funds settlements.
  • Trial by jury on a preponderance of the evidence, with compensatory damages and, against individuals, possibly punitive damages. A judgment against a student may not be collectible.
  • Civil cases of this size typically take two to four years.

Other suits that can follow

  • Defamation. Anything said in the complaint or in court is absolutely privileged. Statements outside court about a named defendant, including editorials, posts and interviews, are not. A defendant who sues would have to prove a false statement of fact, and because this is a matter of public interest, New York's anti-SLAPP law requires proof the statement was made with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard, with fee-shifting if the suit is dismissed (Civil Rights Law §§ 70-a, 76-a). The two defendants found not responsible are the ones for whom out-of-court statements calling all seven rapists are most clearly at issue.
  • Suits against Cornell by respondents. Students found responsible sometimes sue universities over the process (breach of contract, Title IX "erroneous outcome"). One respondent's lawyer has called Cornell's process "biased." None has been filed.

Cornell

  • The independent review. Cornell has agreed to a review by outside counsel at the Governor's request; the reviewer, scope and whether the report will be public have not been announced. Such reviews typically examine what CUPD and administrators did and when, what was sent to the DA, whether the Title IX process followed Cornell's own policy, and what should change. They produce findings and recommendations; any personnel action is Cornell's decision.
  • The Attorney General's review of Cornell. Announced Sept 29, separate from her role as special prosecutor. The office can request records, interview staff, publish findings, and seek a binding agreement or sue if it finds violations of state law, including Education Law Article 129-B. As special prosecutor she can also compel CUPD's records by subpoena. What CUPD sent the DA in 2024, and who decided, is now a question for a prosecutor as well as a reviewer.
  • Federal enforcement. Anyone can file a Title IX complaint with the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights, which can investigate and require a resolution agreement; OCR generally requires complaints within 180 days of the conduct unless it grants a waiver. A Clery Act review can lead to civil fines per violation, currently more than $60,000 each, for failures in crime reporting, timely warnings, or disciplinary-outcome notices.
  • Disclosure. Federal law permits Cornell to name the students it found responsible for a sex offense and their sanctions. It has chosen not to. The Student Assembly's Resolution 15 asks for transparency; Cornell can change its position at any time.
  • CUPD and the Title IX office. Cornell's revised FAQ now says CUPD sent the DA "the victim's sworn statement and the Snapchat screenshot," and the Governor says the transcript's words "never made it into the account that the Cornell police sent to prosecutors." Why, and who decided, are the questions the reviews and the special prosecutor are most likely to answer. Cornell can act on them before the reviews finish.
  • Greek life. The Ithaca mayor has called for a moratorium on student event permits. Cornell's Task Force recommendations, including consent training for Greek members and semi-annual chapter scorecards, are being implemented. Further restrictions are a university decision.
  • Leadership. The Board of Trustees decides the president's tenure. A columnist, the Student Assembly and a petition have called for accountability; the Governor declined on Oct 2 to say whether the president should resign; no trustee action has been reported.

The law

  • The voluntary-intoxication change. The Senate bill passed unanimously in June 2026 and was not taken up by the Assembly. The Assembly working group formed Sept 30 is to report by year end for the session that opens in January 2027, and the Governor said on Oct 2 that she will work to close the gap. If enacted, the definition of "mentally incapacitated" would reach a person incapacitated by substances they took voluntarily, as in Minnesota, Michigan and Texas. It would apply only to conduct after it takes effect.
  • Federal. The Campus Accountability and Safety Act, which Sen. Gillibrand is pressing, would change how colleges handle and report sexual assault. No hearing or vote is scheduled.

The people involved

  • Her. The special prosecutor's office will interview her, which no prosecutor did in 2024. She can testify before the grand jury, where there is no cross-examination, and would be the central witness at any trial, where there is. In the civil case she will be deposed. Her 2024 statement and texts will be used to test her account; the transcript, the Nov 3 text, the rape-kit request and the witnesses' accounts support it. Her attorney said on Oct 1 that his side did not leak the records, and that he was about to ask for a special prosecutor when the Governor appointed one.
  • The seven. Their exposure differs with what each is alleged to have done, which is why this site keeps them separate. Cornell's sanctions stand regardless of the criminal outcome; one respondent's court challenge to his finding has already been denied. Any of them can be a grand jury witness, a defendant, or both. The two found not responsible by Cornell, and the one found responsible only for the attempted offense, face a different question from the four accused of intercourse: whether giving or sharing ketamine, or being in the room, is itself chargeable.
  • The witnesses. The roommates, the big sister, and the other members and guests interviewed by Cornell can be subpoenaed to the grand jury and deposed in the civil case.
  • The records. Part of the Title IX file is now a public court record, filed in one respondent's challenge; the rest reached reporters from unidentified sources. Cornell's procedures give the full investigative file to both parties and their advisors before the hearing. Whether anyone faces consequences depends on what confidentiality terms, if any, the parties agreed to; none has been reported, and her attorney has said his side was not the source.
