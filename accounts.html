What the Title IX records add

Between Sept 29 and Oct 2, 2026, The New York Times, CBS, ABC, NBC and CNN each obtained parts of the record of Cornell's Title IX case: interview transcripts with her, six of the seven respondents and about 20 witnesses (distributed to the parties in January 2025); the panel's 99-page written decision; texts, photographs and emails; the full group chat; and testimony from the 2025 hearings. Part of this is a public court file: one respondent challenged his finding in Tompkins County Supreme Court in March 2026 and the hearing transcript and investigation records were filed there. The records do not change the three accounts in the table above; they add what she said to other people at the time, and what the men said when questioned. Confirmed as to what the records contain, per the outlets that hold them.

What she told the Title IX investigators. Per CBS New York: she did not show "excitement and enthusiasm, which is kinda the basis of consent"; she told the men she was tired and did not want to continue; she was at times encouraged to take more substances; she could not pinpoint when she lost the ability to make decisions; she whispered to one man that she was uncomfortable. On the forum post that circulated in late October, she told investigators it was the first time she "had ever heard the narrative change that I wasn't a whore and that I was assaulted."

What she said at the 2025 hearing. Per ABC News: "I felt like bait. I felt like lions around me. It just was a totally debilitating place to be in." On ketamine: "So, lights are on, but nobody's home. Your brain is not functioning at its proper level." "I know it sounds super weird to say. I know it happened, but I don't remember it."

What her friends told investigators. The morning after: "Oh my goodness, isn't that crazy?" while laughing, per one roommate, who said she seemed in shock and did not call it rape or assault. About a week later a second roommate heard her tell one of the men: "You had penetrative sex with me. I had so much ketamine, my eyes were crossed, and you still had sex with me." One roommate said she "didn't really remember her ever kind of citing that she said yes to anything that occurred"; another said her view of the night shifted "as the days went on." She kept the dress she wore and photographed her bruises.

What it can mean

The texts and the roommates' accounts cut both ways and both sides are already using them. A plaintiff's reading is that denial, self-blame and continued contact with the men are common after sexual assault, that her account moved toward "not consensual" within two weeks, and that she sought a rape kit and reported within three weeks. A defense reading is that her first descriptions, in her own words, were that nothing illegal happened, and that memory and framing changed after rumors spread. Under both Cornell's policy and the Penal Law, what matters is her capacity and expressed will at the time of each act, not what she said afterward; afterward statements go to credibility. The respondents' consistent claim that she did not appear intoxicated is the factual basis for the § 130.10 defense described under Consent, and the Snapchat messages are the evidence most likely to be used against that claim.

What it can mean

Under New York criminal law, the decisive questions are whether she took the drugs knowingly, whether at any point she was unable to communicate unwillingness, and whether any sexual act continued after she clearly said no. The three documents answer those questions differently, which is why the same facts produced "no crime" in 2024 and a grand jury referral in 2026. The DA's position moved within 24 hours from the transcript "changes the landscape" to it "confirms" the sworn statement; what he presents to the grand jury, and on which theory, will show which reading his office has settled on. In the civil case, the 2024 statement and the Oct 21 text will be used to challenge her credibility; the interview transcript, the Nov 3 text, the Snapchat and the bruising will be used to support it. Which document a reader treats as the most reliable is not something this page decides.
